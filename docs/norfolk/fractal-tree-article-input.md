# The Fractal Tree — A Note for Norfolk Botanical Gardens, from Anton Bakker
*First-person input for the journalist's article, focused on the monumental fractal tree.
Reference artifact (not part of the public answer corpus). Companion to the general Norfolk
article and the on-site statements for Koos Knoopje and Reflection.*

---

I want to share the vision for a piece I hope to bring to the gardens: a monumental fractal tree. The design originates with my mentor, Dr. Jacobus Verhoeff, known as Koos. Koos and I built the first bronze version together, and continuing his work at this scale matters a great deal to me. What we usually see as a flat, two-dimensional Pythagorean tree, this piece realizes fully in three dimensions. It branches out into space in every direction, the way a real tree does.

The tree has nine generations of branching. It begins with a single trunk, which divides into two, then four, then eight, and so on, doubling at each generation to a total of 511 branches. Each branch is built from four pieces of cut sheet metal welded together, so the finished tree contains more than two thousand individual pieces. The branches are welded into larger branches, and those into the whole. The structure is built up part by part, which is itself a reflection of how a fractal works: the whole is made of parts that repeat the form of the whole.

Each branch can be read as a vector, a direction in space. Because of the geometry, no two branches point in exactly the same direction, and if the branching continued it would eventually point in infinitely many directions. There is meaning in that. Each branch is unique, yet every one belongs to the same single structure. It is not unlike people: each of us distinct, each pointing our own way, and all of us part of one whole.

The tree also carries a kind of symmetry that often goes unnoticed. We are used to symmetry from side to side, or around a center. This is symmetry across scale. The smallest branches in the ninth generation share the same proportions as the trunk. Look closely at any part and you find the pattern of the whole repeated. This self-similarity is everywhere in nature, but most people pass it by. The tree makes it visible.

Like any tree, this one stands only because of its symmetry and balance. The branching distributes its weight, and the form holds because it is ordered. That is true of a tree, and it is true more broadly of the structures we live within. Placed in a garden, among living trees and plants that branch by the same logic, the sculpture does not present an abstract idea. It points to something already happening in the nature around it. Structure, pattern, beauty, and balance, in the garden and beyond it.

The piece is made of weathering steel. In its current state the welds are visible, but they will not remain so. As the steel weathers and develops its patina, the surface evens out and the joints disappear. Within a fairly short time the tree will read as a single, homogeneous form. The visible seams in the photographs are a stage in the process, not the finished appearance.

A seven-foot version of the tree is already built and ready. I propose we display it in the gardens as a model of the monumental work, at roughly one quarter of the planned size. It would let visitors understand the structure and the craftsmanship at close range while the full piece is being considered. For the monumental version, we are looking at a height of roughly twenty to thirty feet, placed in the existing circular space in the rose garden, which is about seventy five feet in diameter. At that scale a visitor could stand beneath the canopy and look up through the generations of branching.

Anton Bakker
