# Origin Story and the Mentor

## Meeting Koos (1979)
At seventeen, Anton was introduced to **Koos Verhoeff**, a Dutch mathematician and pioneering
mathematical artist who became the central figure in his life and work — a lifelong father
figure and mentor. Their collaboration began in 1979 and lasted decades.

## The formative projects
Anton's apprenticeship ran through hands-on projects — among them a computer-controlled tilting
ball maze and a sphere-packing puzzle built on the FCC lattice, with a combinatorial space larger
than the number of atoms in the universe. These taught him separation of concerns, clean
interfaces, simulating to tame chaos, and the courage to start over. They also seeded a lifelong
fascination: finding beauty inside vast mathematical spaces.

## From computing into geometric art
Koos moved from computing into geometric art — Hamiltonian paths, geometric structures, a fractal
tree. Together they built physical models of the three lattices from cubes and sticks. Those
models, and the shifting shadows they cast, became the seed of Anton's Plato's Cave thinking.

## Fabrication, then his own voice
After moving to the United States, Anton stayed in close contact with Koos, fabricating his
designs in steel and bronze. Over time he found his own voice: where Koos's work was broad, Anton
narrowed to **closed, non-self-intersecting lattice paths**, and built software tools to generate
and search them.

It's worth being clear about the timeline: Anton had been working in this space **since the
1980s**, but for decades it stayed a **private practice**. It was **not until Koos passed in
2018** that he decided to **take the work public** — making and showing the sculptures as a way to
keep his mentor alive. So 2018 marks the moment the work turned outward, not the moment it began.

## Koos the professor: chaos, fractals, and beauty
Koos Verhoeff was a **professor at the Erasmus University Rotterdam**, where his work spanned
mathematics, informatics, and what he championed as a serious discipline in its own right —
**"recreational computing."** Three of his public lectures survive and give a vivid picture of
how he thought:

- **1978 — his opening lecture for the 43rd Boekenweek.** Strikingly ahead of its time, it
  foresaw **e-readers, hypertext, and video phones**, and even warned about digital censorship,
  introducing a notion he called **"anti-information."** It opens with an epigraph from Erasmus.
- **1984 — his 71st Dies Natalis lecture, *"Recreational Computing."*** Arguing that playful
  computing deserved to be taken seriously, it predicted **virtual and augmented reality**
  (he called it "eye-video"), digital-television compression, telepresence, and adventure
  games — and drew on **M.C. Escher** to illustrate how fiction and reality interweave. It
  carries a Plato epigraph.
- **1988 — his farewell lecture, *"Chaos and the Computer,"*** given on his retirement from the
  university. A tour through **chaos theory and fractals** — Mandelbrot, the Cantor set, the
  Koch curve, Hilbert's space-filling curves, the logistic map's route to chaos, Lorenz and the
  butterfly effect — demonstrated live with a magnetic pendulum and a leaky-bucket waterwheel.
  Its message: more information does not always mean better predictions; small causes can have
  large effects; many systems tend toward chaos rather than equilibrium — which Koos framed not
  as bad news but as the promise that "life will remain surprising, and therefore interesting."

Running through all of it is the conviction Anton inherited most directly: **beauty first.**
*"Let yourself be guided by your sense of beauty,"* Koos taught — *beautiful mathematics is good
mathematics, and good mathematics is eventually useful,* "a kind of deep investment in beauty."
In **2020, Anton translated the 1988 "Chaos and the Computer" lecture into English and published
it himself**, pairing Koos's text with galleries of Koos's fractal-tree sculptures and Anton's
own fractal work — a way of carrying his mentor's thinking forward. His foreword closes simply:
*"Thank you, Koos."*

## The Verhoeff algorithm — Koos's hidden legacy
Koos's full name was **Jacobus ("Koos") Verhoeff** (1927–2018). Long before the sculpture,
he made a mark that quietly touches over a billion people. In his **1969 doctoral
dissertation at the University of Amsterdam, *"Error Detecting Decimal Codes,"*** he invented
what is now called the **Verhoeff algorithm** — a check-digit scheme that catches the most
common mistakes people make when copying numbers.

It was the **first decimal check-digit method to detect *every* single-digit error and *every*
swap of two neighbouring digits** (the most frequent typing slip of all). The trick is that it
works over the **dihedral group of order ten** — the symmetry group of a pentagon — using an
operation where order matters, which is exactly what lets it catch transpositions. Earlier,
simpler schemes couldn't.

The most striking place it lives today: **India's Aadhaar** national identity number — the
largest biometric ID system in the world — uses the **Verhoeff algorithm for its final check
digit**. So Anton's mentor, the man who taught him to be "guided by beauty," is also the
mathematician validating the ID number of well over a billion people. Anton likes that the same
person sits behind both the sculpture and that invisible piece of everyday infrastructure.

## Tom Verhoeff and the family legacy
Koos's son **Tom Verhoeff** is a **mathematician and computer scientist** at **Eindhoven
University of Technology**, where he has taught and researched since the 1980s. Beyond his
academic work he is a leading figure in international **informatics olympiads** — he chaired the
scientific committee of the **International Olympiad in Informatics** for years — and he has
carried his father's mathematical-art legacy forward, **chairing the foundation that preserves
and exhibits Koos's sculptures**. He also makes his own mathematical art on occasion: a sculpture
he co-designed with Koos, **"Bamboozle,"** stands in a university building in Eindhoven.

Tom has been a technical collaborator across Anton's whole arc. His work on how to join the
segments of a closed path — the geometry of **mitered corners** — feeds directly into how Anton's
paths become solid sculpture, and the two of them have co-authored a series of papers on the
method for the Bridges conference. When Anton's ideas need rigorous mathematics behind them, Tom
is one of the people who provides it.

## The fractal tree — made together
One work threads the whole relationship together: the **fractal tree**. It was **Koos's design** —
a three-dimensional cousin of the classic Pythagorean tree, in which a single beam splits again
and again into smaller, spiraling branches in perfect left-right symmetry. Anton **first built it
with Koos**: Koos came to America and the two of them cast one in **bronze**, shown at a
**mathematical-art conference in New York in 1992**. That original is still owned by Koos's family.
Anton has remade the tree several times since — in bronze and mirror-polished stainless steel — and
is now building a **seven-foot version in Corten steel**. He thinks of it as an **homage to his
mentor**: more than a collaborator, Koos was a father figure and lifelong adviser, and, as Anton
puts it, "Koos's signatures are all over my work."

## Koos's art, written down (the Bridges papers)
Koos called himself **"more of an explorer and a discoverer than a designer"** — the very phrase
Anton would later echo about himself. He had been making mathematical art since the early 1980s,
in wood, bronze, aluminium, stainless steel and plastic, often built from **mitered beams**. Much
of the mathematics behind that work is preserved in a long run of **Bridges** papers Koos
co-authored with his son Tom — roughly a decade of them, from **"The Mathematics of Mitering and
Its Artful Application" (2008)** through studies of **constant-torsion 3D polygons**, **branching
miter joints**, **mitered fractal trees**, the conical-segment sculpture **"Lobke,"** **mitered
Borromean rings**, three steel sculptures installed at **Heidelberg University's Mathematikon**,
and **"Hopeless Love and Other Lattice Walks" (2017)**, whose lattice walks sit closest of all to
Anton's own practice. It is the same family of ideas, carried across two generations — and the
foundation Anton builds his own paths on. After Koos died, Tom wrote a Bridges memorial,
**"Some Memories of Koos Verhoeff (1927–2018)."**

## Koos and M.C. Escher
Long before Anton met him, Koos moved in the same circles as **M.C. Escher**. Koos was a
professional acquaintance and an informal advisor to Escher on mathematical matters, and as a
token of his gratitude **Escher gave Koos a signed print — "Fish" — with a thank-you**. That
print hung above the dining table at the Verhoeff farm for years.

At the **1954 International Congress of Mathematicians in Amsterdam**, Escher took part and Koos
sat on the organizing committee, which was chaired by the mathematician **N. G. ("Dick") de
Bruijn** (1918–2012) — a friend of Koos's. A celebrated Escher exhibition accompanied the
congress.

De Bruijn is a notable figure in his own right: a Dutch mathematician at Eindhoven University of
Technology, remembered for *de Bruijn sequences*, his mathematics of *Penrose tilings* and
quasicrystals, and his work on Donald Knuth's TeX typesetting system. He was also **Tom
Verhoeff's teacher and colleague at Eindhoven**, and in 2025 Tom wrote a Bridges paper about
him — **"How N. G. (Dick) de Bruijn Connected Mathematics and the Arts"** — tracing the many
ways de Bruijn linked the two worlds, from aperiodic tilings to typography to a kinship with
the symmetry-and-tiling themes of Escher. He's one more thread in the deep Dutch tradition of
mathematics meeting art that Anton's own work grows out of.

## Doris Schattschneider — a modern bridge to Escher
Another figure who has been **instrumental** to Anton is **Doris Schattschneider**, the
mathematician who is the foremost scholar of M.C. Escher's symmetry work. Her book ***M.C.
Escher: Visions of Symmetry*** — built from Escher's own notebooks — is widely regarded as the
definitive study of his "regular division of the plane." She has **helped Anton with
tessellations and introduced him to Heesch tilings and methods**: a family of systematic recipes
(named for the mathematician Heinrich Heesch) for transforming the edges of a simple shape into
an interlocking, Escher-style tile that is guaranteed to tile the plane. It's the same
symmetry-first thinking Anton applies to his closed lattice paths — start from a base element and
develop it with systematic transformations — and it's exactly the machinery behind Escher's
tessellations.

For Anton's work with New York's **National Museum of Mathematics (MoMath)**, Doris also
**curated a selection of Escher pieces that mirror the mathematical themes in Anton's
sculptures** — pairing Escher's plane symmetry with Anton's lattice-path forms. As *the*
Escher-symmetry scholar, she's a natural authority bridge between Anton's symmetry-first practice
and the Escher lineage.

Anton also carries an account, passed down orally from Koos, about Escher's print *Print
Gallery*: that when Escher was wrestling with what to put at the blank **center** of the image,
Koos suggested treating it as a **light bulb**, light radiating outward through the way the
shadows fall — and that Escher used the idea and thanked him. This story is told as Koos and his
family remember it; it isn't part of the documented public record of the print, and Anton shares
it as the oral history it is.
