# Escher's 1963 lectures: texts, agendas, and a comparison

This covers the two lectures M.C. Escher gave at the **Mathematisch Centrum (Mathematical Centre),
Amsterdam**, in 1963: the two short **introduction texts** (Map M 106, M 107) translated in full, the
two detailed **agendas** (Map M 108a, M 108b) rendered as outlines, and a **comparison** of how the
two evenings are built. The Dutch originals are in **Koos Verhoeff's archive** and are held as scans
in the media library. The introductions are Escher's own words, translated for research; the agendas
are renderings of his dense handwritten outlines (a few entries are abbreviated or uncertain in the
original). The material remains under the copyright of the M.C. Escher estate.

For why these matter to Koos's story (he helped reshape the second lecture, and Escher thanked him
with the inscribed *Fish* print the day after), see "Koos Verhoeff and M.C. Escher."

## Foreword, first lecture (29 October 1963) — Map M 106

Chairman, ladies and gentlemen,

It is a great pleasure for me, and I consider it an honour, that the Mathematical Centre has invited
me to give two lectures about my work.

I myself know almost nothing of theoretical mathematics, but it is a fact that my contacts with
mathematicians, and especially with crystallographers, have steadily grown in recent years.

I seem to find myself in a borderland, a no-man's-land. On one side lies the domain my work formerly
belonged to, the one I emerged from: the visual arts; on the other side lies mathematics. With one
foot I perhaps still stand in the territory of art, while I have set my other foot, hesitantly, in the
field of mathematics.

This first evening I should like to devote wholly and exclusively to my applications of the regular
division of the plane, leaning now and then heavily on the crystallographers, at least those who
concern themselves with symmetries in the plane.

The following week I hope to give an overview of the rest of my hobbies, in which spherical forms,
regular polyhedra, relativities, inversions, and more such playful notions will come up.

But for this evening, then, only the regular division of the plane. It has in any case been by far
the richest source of inspiration I have ever tapped, and I can easily fill a whole evening with it.

What I understand by the regular division of the plane, or regular filling of the plane, I can best
illustrate directly with slides.

## Introduction, second lecture (5 November 1963, after the break) — Map M 107

I do not know whether you, as a child, were as fond as I was of the fairy tales of Grimm.

Among them was one that intrigued me intensely as a boy of about ten, and that I reread again and
again. It was called, if I remember rightly, "The Credulous King."

It was about a king who had a beautiful daughter. Every suitor who came to court to vie for the
princess's hand first had to tell the king a story, and he promised that he would marry his daughter
to whoever told him a story so unbelievable that he would cry out, "No, that can't be!!"

You can well imagine how it went: a whole row of princes told the most fantastic stories, but when
they had finished the king always said, "It is possible." Until at last a farm boy came, clever
enough to serve up a tale about the king's grandfather, who had once pulled off a giant villainous
trick. The king was so wounded in his family pride that he cried out, "No, that can't be!!", and as a
result he had to give his daughter to the clever Hans.

That "punch line" never quite satisfied me. It was too "psychological" for me. The impossibility in
question was not real enough for me. For I believe that an impossible situation only truly catches the
eye when the impossibility does not reveal itself at first glance.

If you want to draw attention to something impossible, you must try first to fool yourself, and then
your audience, by serving up the story in such a way that the element of impossibility is veiled, so
that a superficial listener does not even notice it. There must be a certain enigmatic quality that
does not immediately leap to the eye.

I have noticed that mathematicians have a playful fondness for such stories, and several of the
"impossible objects" I am about to show you were influenced by them, came about through suggestions
they gave me.

## Detailed agenda, first lecture (29 October 1963) — Map M 108a
*"Regular Division of the Plane" (the same outline was reused at the Academie van Bouwkunst,
Amsterdam, on 21 March 1964). A dense, exhaustively slide-numbered taxonomy organized by formal and
symmetry category.*

**I. Before the break**
- **① Introduction.** 1a Primal forms (honeycomb; insect eye; fish and reptile scales; chessboard,
  tiled floors). 1b Systems (translation; axes; glide reflection). 2a Eight abstract examples from
  applied art (Japanese/Chinese; Russian icon vestments; Moorish, the Mezquita of Córdoba; Moorish,
  the Alhambra), all abstract and unrecognizable, one motif, no glide reflection. 2b Towers
  (recognizable natural form; "the recognizability of the motifs is important to me!"). 3a Rearing
  horse (one asymmetric motif). 3b Boats and fish (two alternating motifs). 4a Butterflies / 4b
  Reptiles (2-, 3-, 6-fold axes). 5a/5b Swans (glide reflection). 6a/6b Horseman (glide reflection;
  "an exceptionally developed motif"). 7a Plane-filling I (6×6, 18 light / 18 dark, 4 nodal points) /
  7b Plane-filling II (no system; 14 light / 21 dark).
- **② Developments in form and contrast** (the gray plane as starting point). 8a Twelve stages / 8b
  "Liberation" (flat to spatial). 9a "Development I" (gray to white-and-black) / 9b "Verbum"
  (inside-out).
- **③ Function and meaning of backgrounds.** 10a/10b Birds and fish, to "Sky and Water I". 11a/11b
  Birds, to "Day and Night". 12 "Sun and Moon".

**II. After the break**
- **④ Endlessness / the infinitely large number.** 13a "Development II" (arbitrary boundary, open
  composition) / 13b "Path of Life I" (logical boundary, closed). 14a scheme of one-quarter of 14b /
  14b "Smaller and Smaller" (four conditions: end-grain boxwood; sharp burins; a steady hand; a
  magnifying lens). 15a "Whirlpools" (two cores; one block for red and gray) / 15b spherical surface
  with fish (8 spiral axes). 16a/16b Fish; beechwood sphere, 12 congruent fish. 17a/17b Angels and
  devils; maple sphere, 12 + 12. 18a "Circle Limit IV" (Heaven and Hell) / 18b "Circle Limit III"
  (Coxeter, *Introduction to Geometry*).
- **⑤ "Picture-stories": from the plane to suggestions of spatial form.** 19a little men / 19b
  "Cycle". 20a birds and fish / 20b "Predestination". 21a little lions / 21b "Magic Mirror". 22a
  little men / 22b "Encounter". 23a reptiles / 23b "Reptiles" (reincarnation). 24a "Metamorphosis I"
  / 24b "Metamorphosis II" (→ reptiles → honeycomb bees). 25a "Metamorphosis III" / 25b "Metamorphosis
  IV". 26a "Metamorphosis V" (→ houses → city → tower → chess) / 26b "Metamorphosis VI" (→ chessboard
  → the letters of "metamorphose").

## Detailed agenda, second lecture (5 November 1963) — Map M 108b
*"Possibilities, Realities." Conceptually architected: a thesis (impossibility veiled as recognizable
reality), the Grimm fairy tale as the after-break opener, and a deliberate build to the "Impossible
Objects" climax. It foregrounds the mathematicians' role throughout.*

**I. Before the break: "Possibilities, Realities"**
- **① Depictions of observed realities.** 1a "Castrovalva" 1930 / 1b "St. Peter's" 1935 (three
  vanishing points). 2a "Hand with Reflecting Sphere" 1935 (the ego at the center of the universe) /
  2b "Three Spheres II" 1946. 3a "Eye" 1946 (a personal element: death) / 3b "Dewdrop" 1948. Water
  reflections: 4a "Puddle" 1952 / 4b "Rippled Surface" 1950. 5a "Three Worlds" 1955 (leaves;
  reflected trees; fish below).
- **② Depictions of personal invention.** 6a "Dream" 1935 (the Ravello church; a sarcophagus; a
  praying mantis) / 6b "Tower of Babel" 1928. 7a "Cubic space-filling" / 7b "Concentric
  space-filling". Regular solids: 8a "Depth" / 8b "Stars". 9a star-dodecahedron, 12 starfish / 9b five
  tetrahedra, twelve flower-cups. 10a/10b "Tetrahedron-planetoid" and a detail. 11a "Double Planet" /
  11b "Flatworms" (from Buchsbaum, *Animals Without Backbones*; *Worm Runner's Digest*, University of
  Michigan). 12a "Order and Chaos" / 12b "Gravity" (reused after the break as 20a).
- **②b Ribbons.** 13a "Spirals" (torus) / 13b "Sphere-spirals". 14a "Covering" (Wells, *The Invisible
  Man*) / 14b "Band" (endless). 15a "Möbius Band I" / 15b "Möbius Band II" (with ants).

**II. After the break: impossibilities presented as recognizable realities**
- **③ Conflict between flat and spatial.** 16a "Doric Columns" / 16b "Three Spheres I". 17a "Drift"
  (reading uncertain) / 17b "Drawing" (Saul Steinberg, *All in a Line*). 18a "Balcony" 1945 / 18b
  "Print Gallery" 1956 (a perspective progression by a factor of 256; van Wijngaarden and van Dantzig,
  1957: "a Riemann surface!").
- **④ Inversion.** 19a "Concave and Convex" 1955 (made at the suggestion of Prof. Schouten) / 19b
  "Cube with Ribbons" 1957.
- **⑤ Relativity of the function of a plane.** 20a "Gravity" 1952 / 20b "Other World" 1947 (wall =
  floor = ceiling). 21a "High and Low" 1947 / 21b "Relativity" 1953 (three mutually perpendicular
  gravities). 22a "Curl-up" (*Wentelteefje*) 1951 (von Koenigswald's phacops fossil; Casimir) / 22b
  "House of Stairs" 1951 (glide reflections).
- **⑥ "Impossible Objects"** (English in the original). 23a a detail of "Belvedere" (van Dantzig's
  ground-sketch of the fourth dimension, 1956) / 23b "Belvedere" 1958. 24a Prof. L.S. Penrose's
  "continuous staircase" / 24b "Ascending and Descending" 1960. 25a Dr. R. Penrose's "triangle" (sent
  late 1961) / 25b "Waterfall" 1961 (the Penrose triangle three times over; a perpetuum mobile).

## How the two lectures differ
The two evenings are built very differently, and that contrast is the heart of why Anton and Tom
Verhoeff read **Koos's hand** in the second one.

**The introductions.** The first lecture's foreword (M 106) is plain and modest: a courteous
thank-you, the remark that he stands "in a borderland between art and mathematics," and a flat
announcement of the topic. It opens an exposition, with no hook and no thesis. The second lecture's
introduction (M 107) is built like a story: it opens with a childhood memory (the Grimm tale "The
Credulous King"), turns it into a thesis (an impossibility only works when it is veiled and does not
reveal itself at first glance), and credits the mathematicians whose suggestions shaped the
"impossible objects." It is narrative, argued, and aimed at a destination.

**The agenda structures.** The first agenda (M 108a) is an exhaustive, slide-by-slide **taxonomy**
ordered by formal and symmetry category: primal forms, systems, motifs, glide reflections,
developments of contrast, the function of backgrounds, then endlessness and the picture-stories. It
is comprehensive and flat, a catalog, and it reflects Escher's own characteristic way of ordering his
material. The second agenda (M 108b) is an **argument that builds to a climax**: from depictions of
observed reality, to personal invention, to the conflict between flat and spatial, to inversion, to
the relativity of a plane's function, and finally to the "Impossible Objects" as the payoff, with the
mathematicians' role kept in view throughout.

**The pattern.** The first lecture catalogs; the second argues and builds to a climax. The first
states a topic; the second tells a story with a thesis. That shift, from taxonomy to a deliberately
architected narrative, is the structural change Anton and Tom read as Koos's contribution, and it is
the help for which Escher gave Koos the inscribed *Fish* print the next day.
