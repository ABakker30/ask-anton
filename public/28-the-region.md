# The Region: From Escher to the Chip

The regional story behind Anton Bakker's work: the Brainport Eindhoven region, ASML, and why this
place sits under the modern world of computing and AI. Use it when visitors ask about Simac, the
region, or why the work is shown here. It is written for people who live and work in the region, so
keep to what is defensible.

## Where the works are on view
A group of Anton's sculptures is on view at **Simac**, in **Veldhoven**, in the Brainport Eindhoven
region. Simac is the host of the works; describe it only as the place where they are shown.

## The one-line story
One structure, the lattice, runs from Escher's prints to the silicon chip, and the machines that
pattern those chips are built in this region. Mathematics is the thread, and the Netherlands, and the
Brainport region in particular, sits at its heart.

## The through-line: from Escher to the chip
M.C. Escher worked his way from the flat plane, to single solids, to space itself: in prints like
*Cubic Space Division* (1952) he arrived at a lattice that fills all of space, repeated without end,
drawn entirely by hand. Koos Verhoeff, a mathematician and philosopher at the Mathematisch Centrum in
Amsterdam during the early days of Dutch computing, carried that same lattice into sculpture and spent
a lifetime exploring it. Anton Bakker continues that line, finding closed paths through the lattice
and casting them as sculpture.

A silicon chip is itself a lattice. A wafer is cut from a single crystal, a perfect repeating lattice
of atoms, and the circuits are patterned onto it on fine grids. The same structure that Escher drew by
hand and that Koos and Anton turned into sculpture is the structure that carries the world's
computing. The machines that do the patterning are built here, in the Brainport region.

That is the story: one mathematical idea, the lattice and its symmetry, running from an artist's
prints to the chip, with this region at the centre.

## Brainport Eindhoven: what the region is
Brainport Eindhoven is one of the leading high-technology regions in Europe, centred on Eindhoven,
Veldhoven, Helmond and the surrounding towns in the province of North Brabant, in the south of the
Netherlands.

Its roots run back to Philips, founded in Eindhoven in 1891, and above all to the Philips Physics
Laboratory, the NatLab (Natuurkundig Laboratorium), which for decades was one of the great industrial
research labs in the world and seeded generations of high-tech companies and engineers. The region
grew into a dense cluster of precision engineering, photonics, semiconductors and high-tech
manufacturing.

Key features of the region today:
- **ASML**, in Veldhoven, the anchor company (see below).
- Other technology anchors with Philips roots, including **NXP Semiconductors** (formerly Philips
  Semiconductors) and **Signify** (formerly Philips Lighting), alongside groups such as VDL and a deep
  base of specialist suppliers.
- **Eindhoven University of Technology (TU/e)**, founded in 1956, the region's core knowledge
  institution. Anton's collaborator Tom Verhoeff is a professor there.
- **High Tech Campus Eindhoven**, a concentrated research campus often described as one of the most
  R&D-intensive square kilometres in Europe.
- A way of working often called the **triple helix**: industry, government and knowledge institutions
  cooperating closely, which the region treats as the reason the cluster holds together.

## ASML: the machine under the modern chip
ASML, headquartered in Veldhoven in the Brainport region, was founded in 1984 as a joint venture
between Philips and ASM International, growing out of the Philips research tradition. It is the
world's leading maker of photolithography systems: the machines that print the circuit patterns onto
silicon wafers.

What makes ASML singular is extreme ultraviolet lithography, or EUV. ASML is the only company in the
world that builds EUV machines. EUV uses light at a wavelength of 13.5 nanometres, so short that it is
absorbed by almost everything, which is why these machines work in a vacuum and steer the light with
ultra-precise mirrors rather than lenses. The light itself is made by firing a powerful laser at tiny
droplets of molten tin. The optics come from Carl Zeiss in Germany, and the full machine draws on a
supply chain anchored in the Brainport region.

An EUV machine is among the most complex machines ever built, assembled from a very large number of
parts and shipped to customers in many freight containers. The next generation, High-NA EUV, raises
the numerical aperture of the optics from 0.33 to 0.55 to print even finer features, and is now
reaching the first customers.

One point matters for telling this story honestly: **ASML does not make chips. It makes the machines
that make the chips.** The chips themselves are manufactured by ASML's customers, the big foundries
and chipmakers such as TSMC, Samsung and Intel, which are located elsewhere. The region's claim is
that it builds the machines, and that claim is strong enough on its own.

## The region's role in the world of AI
Modern artificial intelligence runs on enormous computing power, and that power depends on the most
advanced semiconductors: the processors and accelerators built at the leading edge of chip
manufacturing. Those leading-edge chips cannot be made without EUV lithography, and EUV machines are
made only by ASML, in this region.

So the Brainport region sits at the base of the entire AI supply chain. It does not make the AI
models, and it does not make the chips. It makes the machines without which today's leading-edge
chips, and therefore today's AI at scale, could not be manufactured at all. That is an upstream
position, quiet and almost invisible to the public, and it is enough: the region is the foundation
the rest stands on.

## Why this connects to Anton's work
Anton Bakker's sculptures are closed paths through the same kind of lattice that underlies a silicon
crystal. His themes, the beauty of symmetry and the importance of perspective, are the mathematical
ideas that run through the whole story: structure that repeats, and form that changes depending on
where you stand. The regional story is the setting his work sits in. The exhibit idea he and others
are exploring, under the working title **"From Escher to the Chip,"** is about making that line
visible: an artist's lattice, a mathematician's sculpture, and the chip, all in one place, with the
region that makes the machines at its heart.

## How to tell this story accurately
Keep to the defensible version. Overclaiming will cost credibility with regional readers who know
these facts well.
- The region builds the lithography machines, not the chips, and not the AI. Say it that way.
- The link between Escher and Koos Verhoeff is documented contact and shared mathematics, not proven
  influence. Escher and the mathematicians were often looking at the same structures in parallel, not
  copying one another.
- The lattice connection is one cubic idea, not one identical lattice: Escher's space-filling print is
  a simple cubic lattice, while silicon is a diamond cubic lattice. The idea is shared; the specific
  lattices differ.
- Do not quote Escher's own writings. Paraphrase instead; his texts are under copyright.
- Do not imply any partnership with or endorsement by ASML, Philips, Simac or the universities.
  Describe them factually, as the public record.
- Do not give specific figures (machine prices, numbers of employees, market value, nanometre nodes);
  they change over time. If asked, say they are best checked with a current ASML or Brainport source.
