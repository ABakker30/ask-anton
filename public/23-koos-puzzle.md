# The Koos Puzzle

## What it is
The **Koos Puzzle** is a sphere-packing puzzle built on the **FCC (face-centered cubic)
lattice** — the same lattice that underlies Anton's sculpture. There are **25 unique pieces,
named A through Y**, and each piece is a cluster of **four connected spheres** in FCC
adjacency. One full set is 100 spheres, and the challenge is to assemble them into a target
shape: a stacked 5×5×4 arrangement, a pyramid, or any of countless other forms.

The numbers get out of hand almost immediately. The 25 pieces have **475 distinct
orientations** between them — from 2 for the most symmetric piece to 48. Computed exactly
from the piece data, the classic 100-sphere shape admits **nearly 10⁹³ ways to attempt an
assembly — over a trillion times the number of atoms in the observable universe** (~10⁸⁰),
and the largest shapes push past 10¹⁰⁰. It was Anton's first encounter with a lifelong
theme: finding beauty inside vast mathematical spaces.

Anton named the puzzle for his mentor, **Koos Verhoeff**. Today it lives on as a free web
app at **koospuzzle.com**.

## Origin: the Ball Puzzle Project (early 1980s)
The puzzle grew directly out of Anton's apprenticeship with Koos. After the Ball Maze
Project — the computer-controlled tilting maze they built together in the late 1970s — the
two began the **Ball Puzzle Project** in the early 1980s: many balls forming shapes, pieces
defined as 4-sphere clusters in FCC packing, and the central problem of counting each
piece's valid orientations within the lattice. Computing was severe in those days —
assembly-language era, hand-built machines — which forced careful representation.

## The Hollow Pyramid
The most legendary target shape was the **Hollow Pyramid**. For many years nobody knew
whether it could be solved at all, and a **1,000 guilder reward** was publicly attached to
it. One long computer run in search of a solution ended dramatically: a power-supply failure
filled the historic farm building with smoke and nearly burned it down. (This is told as
Anton and the Verhoeff family remember it.)

The Hollow Pyramid does have a solution. It was eventually found by **two brothers who wrote
solver programs** of their own; beyond that, the details that survive are limited. The years
of not knowing are a large part of why the shape became legendary.

## The revival
Decades later the puzzle came back to life. Around the **Bridges 2025 conference in the
Netherlands**, renewed community interest — including an email from someone who had taken up
the Hollow Pyramid problem in retirement — collided with a question Anton had long wanted to
answer better: *why do you work in FCC and related lattices?* The puzzle **is** that answer:
it is where the fascination began, years before the sculpture.

So Anton rebuilt it as a modern experience: **koospuzzle.com**, a free app that runs in the
browser and installs on a phone. There you can solve puzzles by hand, watch solver engines
attack the combinatorics, design and share your own target shapes, play against the
computer, and challenge others to beat your result. He is also exploring a manufactured
physical edition.

## How it connects to the artwork
The puzzle and the sculpture are two expressions of one practice. The FCC lattice that holds
the puzzle's spheres is the same structure Anton's closed paths travel through; the
orientation-counting and symmetry questions the puzzle raised in the 1980s are the same
machinery behind the path searches today. And the puzzle carries the philosophy Koos taught
most directly — **play is a serious form of learning**, and discovery beats memorization.
Solving a shape nobody has solved before is, in miniature, the same act as finding a path
nobody has walked.
