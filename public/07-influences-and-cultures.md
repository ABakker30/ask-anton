# Influences and Cultures

Anton's travels keep showing him the same thing: the patterns he explores are not new. Cultures
around the world discovered them long ago — almost always in two dimensions — and held them as
sacred, as beautiful, as fundamental to order. What he does is carry that ancient practice into
**three dimensions**. If beauty and order live in flat patterns, he asks, what happens when you
fully explore them in space?

## India — rangoli
In Indian villages Anton watched women make **rangoli**: intricate geometric and floral designs
drawn on the ground before homes for festivals, in colored powders and flower petals. The
practice is spiritual — it welcomes good fortune — and deliberately **temporary**, swept away
after a day, a quiet lesson in impermanence. What struck him most was the method: the makers lay
down a **grid of dots first, then draw flowing curves through those points** — exactly what Anton
does when he turns a straight-edged lattice path into a curved one. And they develop their motifs
by **repeating, reflecting, and reversing** — the very moves he calls "music for the eyes." People
who had never heard of his work were doing the same thing, intuitively, for generations.

## Morocco — Berber carpets
In the geometric weavings of the **Amazigh (Berber)** people he saw the same shapes — diamonds,
crosses, zigzags — but kept **angular** rather than curved. The uncanny part: those woven shapes
are the very ones that appear when Anton **projects one of his three-dimensional sculptures onto a
flat plane, or lets it cast a shadow**. The weavers found the patterns through their craft; he
finds them by exploring the lattice. Same geometry, different dimension.

## Tibet — the endless knot
The Tibetan Buddhist **endless knot** — a single line with no beginning or end — moved him, and
led to one of his most meaningful sculptures (see *"Awakening"* in the stories). Buddhism's sense
of interconnection sits close to his own philosophy of perspective.

## Spain and Mexico
He has seen **Islamic geometric tilework** in Spain — the same tradition that captivated **M.C.
Escher** on his early travels and fed straight into Escher's art (Anton has *not* visited the
Alhambra itself, but knows that lineage well). In **Mexico**, the ancient **Maya** sites showed
him familiar geometry in both their carvings and their architecture. A pattern emerged across all
these places: what survives is mostly **flat, symbolic ornament**, while three-dimensional
geometry tended to be **structural** — buildings, pyramids. Pure geometric beauty as a
free-standing sculpture, drawn from those ancient principles, is the space Anton works in.

## The thread
From rangoli to the Alhambra to the mandala, every culture that looked closely at beauty and order
found the same truths. Anton sees himself continuing that long human lineage — with new tools, and
in three dimensions — which is part of what he means by **Perspectivism**.
