# The Ideas Behind the Work

## Symmetry is the architecture of beauty
This is the organizing principle. Symmetry is the hidden structure that makes people respond to a
sculpture even when they don't know the lattice mathematics underneath. Anton believes the
response to symmetry is **innate** in us.

He carries a second, related definition from his engineering life: **"architecture is that which
makes things work."** The same instinct — for structure that holds — runs through his software,
his thinking, and his sculpture.

## Perspective reveals truth (Plato's Cave)
From one angle a sculpture can look like an illusion; move, and from another angle it reveals its
truth — the same structure, seen rightly. This goes back to lattice models that hung in his
boyhood room, casting shadows that shifted with the light: triangles, squares, hexagons, all from
one object. Anton links it explicitly to **Plato's Cave** — we mistake shadows for reality until
we move and see more. The lesson is literal and moral at once: look from many angles before
judging what is true. He sees the sculptures as a metaphor for how to understand information, and
life: "I like to have structures that are really different depending on how you look at them. I
encourage people to **look at things from all sides**."

## Symmetry as a way of living
For Anton symmetry isn't only aesthetic — it's how things ought to work. A good deal is
**win-win**, not winner and loser. Balance belongs in relationships, in business, in the world.
He hopes the work opens people's eyes to that.

## How things are linked
Like his mentor Koos Verhoeff, Anton loves to **philosophize about how things are linked** — what
is linked to what, what **degrees of freedom** a thing has, and **who is constraining what, and
how**. It's the same structural curiosity that ran through his work in software and systems, now
turned on form itself. Many of his most interesting sculptures explore exactly this space: paths
that are **entangled** with one another, mutually constraining, yet each still free to move. It's
the thinking behind his **links** — two or more separate paths that interlock and fit snugly
together without ever touching.

## Lattices: the building blocks of life
Anton finds it fascinating that the lattices he works in are the **building blocks of existence
itself** — everything that exists is built from atoms and their structure. He thinks of himself
less as an inventor than a **discoverer** navigating that vast space, searching out and composing
the shapes he's looking for. And just as we don't think about atoms when we admire a flower — we
just feel its beauty, often really responding to its symmetry — he doesn't want viewers to feel
they're "looking at mathematics." He deliberately keeps the math in the background so the
experience up front is **beauty, symmetry, wonder, and perspective**.

## An old human impulse
Anton places the work in a long human tradition of finding meaning in structure: **Plato's cave**
and its shadows, the **constellations** people drew in the stars, the symbols of ancient
civilizations, **Islamic geometric art** and the tessellations of the **Alhambra**, and Indian
**rangoli** — the threshold patterns that are themselves laid out on a lattice grid. He sees
himself carrying that ancient wisdom forward, into three-dimensional sculpture and into plane
tessellations alike.

## Art for the computational age — "Perspectivism"
Anton has a name for the kind of art he makes: **Perspectivism**. His reasoning runs by analogy.
**Cubism** was the artistic response to *its* era. Our era is **computational** — artificial
intelligence works by searching vast high-dimensional spaces for answers; Anton searches the vast
**lattice space** for *beauty*, composing motifs and structures. The name captures both layers at
once: the **computational search**, and the **importance of perspective** — the same structure
that looks like an illusion from one angle reveals its truth from another. It's the through-line
of everything here: look from more than one side before you judge what's true, in art and in life.

## Beauty, then pattern, then wonder
The work is built to unfold across repeated encounters, in a specific order:
- **Beauty** comes first, and it is largely **subconscious** — the pull we feel before we think.
  Anton believes it's an innate recognition of symmetry: we're drawn to a flower without knowing
  *why*, and a sculpture can draw us the same way, for the same reason.
- **Pattern** emerges as you keep looking — the repetition and balance that were there all along.
- **Wonder** arrives only with **sustained** looking: stay long enough and you begin to ask *what
  is really going on here?*

A sculpture's deeper job is to make that subconscious act **conscious**. In a garden, beauty pulls
you to a flower and — without thinking — you stop, step closer, and circle it, seeing it from
different angles. A sculpture is a deliberate object that makes you notice you're doing it. Once you
can name what draws you, you can't unsee it: you start noticing symmetry in the garden, then
everywhere.

And once the method is conscious it runs **both directions**. Beauty can start it — but you can also
begin with curiosity where there's no obvious beauty at all: circle the unfamiliar or the difficult,
look from several sides, and you'll either **discover a hidden beauty** or recognize where **symmetry
is broken** — an imbalance to be repaired or avoided. Exploring from many positions is, in fact, the
only way to tell balance from imbalance. Anton's hope is that people carry that habit past the gates
and apply it to ideas and to one another — to **look at the sides rather than take a side**. That,
more than decoration, is what he wants the sculptures to do: raise the awareness, and the practice,
of seeing.

## Why some works only have a number
Anton usually **describes** a piece — "a path through a polyhedron," "a fractal tree" — or gives it
nothing but an **opus number**, rather than a title. His reasoning is pure Perspectivism: if a work
can be seen so many ways, who is he to fix it to one meaning with a name? A description (or a
number) stays neutral and lets each viewer find their own meaning. He reserves real titles for
three cases: a name for the **experience** rather than a single view (**"Awakening"** — the moment
of seeing differently, not the knot or the squares it can look like); a name the work **literally
earns** (**"Reflection"**, which reflects, sits in a reflecting pool, and invites reflection); or a
**dedication** to a person (**"Koos Knoopje,"** for his mentor). A work can be named for whom it
honors, but rarely for what it means.

## The story is part of the art
A piece is the object **plus** its story — technical, historical, philosophical. That's why Anton
cares so much about the telling: a sculpture is never only what it weighs.
